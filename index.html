Paper Trading Agent

Built properly, measured honestly, and corrected in public. No edge has been established. This site is the record of that result, of the flaw that changed it, and of the machinery built to establish both.

9 real-LLM backtests, 2 of them corrected reruns 41,701-example fine-tuning dataset Deployed to AWS, then destroyed Never touched real capital V2 in progress — risk engine & explainer shipped V3 in planning — Indian markets

What is it

A daily trading decision-support agent. It reads a stock’s recent price history and news headlines, has four specialist components argue about them, and produces one paper trade — every decision, every opinion and every simulated fill recorded and auditable.

It was built to close six engineering gaps at once: a backend API, a real relational database, agentic orchestration, LLM fine-tuning, containerisation with CI/CD, and cloud deployment. Building one system rather than six tutorials forces the parts to interoperate and produces decisions that only arise when components have to live together.

The second half of the project was spent trying to establish whether it actually works. As first measured, it did not — and five separate attempts to improve it produced five measured negative results, each of which says something specific about why. A V2 check then found a flaw in those measurements; corrected, the system no longer trails buy-and-hold, though nothing yet shows an edge.

Nothing here ever touched real money. Trades are simulated against historical prices with slippage modelled; the account is fictional by design.

The finding

A V2 check found a flaw in V1’s backtests. Each decision could see that day’s own closing price — a look-ahead leak. It is fixed, together with two smaller issues found alongside it: decisions made on market holidays, and fills at a price already gone by the open.

As originally published, every backtest longer than a single quarter underperformed buy-and-hold, by $39 to $225 on a 20-share basis, and the out-of-sample run lost $63 in a flat market. Two runs have been redone with the fixes so far, and both land above buy-and-hold: by $83 in-sample and $64 out-of-sample. That is two to three times the $24–34 spread between V1’s repeat runs, so it is not just model randomness. It is also one run per window, on one stock, with four things changed at once — not enough to establish an edge. What the correction does overturn is V1’s “underperforms buy-and-hold”. No edge is established; whether one exists is open again.

Rerun partly done. Runs #3, #4 and #7 have not been redone yet; their figures below still come from the leaky runs. The redone runs also used a different language model (Claude in place of GPT-4o), so a change in result cannot be pinned on the leak alone.

The project’s founding rule was that an honestly evaluated result is the correct thing to report, including when a correction moves it. So it is reported, and the evaluation discipline that caught it is the actual deliverable.

The money, precisely

There was no starting balance

This is worth stating plainly, because every table above reports dollars and it would be reasonable to assume a portfolio behind them. There isn’t one. V1 has no capital-allocation model at all — deliberately, because the goal was to test whether the decision logic had an edge, not to also design position sizing.

What exists instead is a hard cap: 20 shares per symbol, enforced by the risk layer, with each trade sized at up to 10 shares. So the figures are absolute profit and loss on a position that could never exceed 20 shares of one stock.

The percentages above are computed against the capital you would need to run it — 20 shares at the window’s opening price. That is the honest denominator: it is what has to be available for the strategy to be executable at all.

How it works

Four nodes, split by whether judgment is actually required. A Technical Analyst computes RSI-14 and SMA-20 in plain Python — arithmetic is not worth a language model. A Sentiment Analyst reads headlines through a small language model. A Portfolio Manager on a larger one synthesises both into a proposal. A Risk Manager holds final authority and can veto or scale any trade: V1’s hard position rules, plus, since V2, a budget on the position’s value-at-risk.

V1 ran on GPT-4o-mini and GPT-4o. Since V2 the same two nodes run on Claude Haiku 4.5 and Sonnet 5.5 — a configuration setting, not a code change, and every run records which models produced it.

That last split — propose versus gate — was the most valuable structural decision in the project. Because the risk layer is code and every input it consumed is stored, alternative risk rules could later be replayed against recorded decisions for free, without a single new API call.

What’s next — V2, then V3

In progress — V2

V2 replaces the rule-based Risk Manager with real risk math, and adds an agent that explains its own alerts. In place of just a hard position cap, the risk layer computes VaR and CVaR, watches for a shift in how the watchlist’s holdings move together, and backtests its own risk estimate against what actually happened — a Kupiec test, the same kind of check that caught this project’s own pre-registration flaw in Phase 04.

Phase 07, the risk engine, has shipped — with a mixed result, reported as measured. The value-at-risk forecasts hold up: over the in-sample year they were breached on 4.4% of days against a 5% target, while a naive flat 2% estimate was breached on 10.3% and fails the test. Out of sample they were too cautious, breached on only 1.6% of days. Used as a binding, pre-registered 2% budget, the risk engine cost $492 against V1’s rules in-sample and gained $57 out-of-sample. The same rule moving results in opposite directions on two windows is no evidence that it helps. The checks that found the look-ahead leak above were the first step of this phase.

Phase 08, the explainer, has shipped too. When the risk engine cuts or blocks a trade, it explains why in plain language from the headlines around the losses its VaR is built from, and every citation must be a headline it was actually shown. On 20 recorded decisions all 20 explanations were fully grounded, at $0.06 in total. Grounded is not the same as right: in a spot check, one cause in five stretched its headline, which is what the next phase grades.

Still to come: every explanation graded against a hand-labelled set of past events before it is trusted — including whether full-text retrieval actually beats plain recency, and whether the language model beats a no-news template stating the same facts — and every retrieval and reasoning step traced, not just the output.

This does not depend on the strategy having an edge. It makes the existing system’s judgment better and its reasoning visible — the same gap the rest of this project already cares about.

In planning — V3

V3 moves to Indian markets — NSE and BSE. Not as a continuation of a strategy with no established edge, but because it asks a different question: does the same pipeline behave differently on a different market, with different liquidity, different news flow and a different retail-participation profile?

The architecture is ready for it. Market data and news arrive through swappable protocols rather than hard-coded clients, so an Indian data source is a new implementation of an existing interface, not a rewrite.

What has already been checked, for V3

Zerodha, Upstox, Angel One and Fyers all publish free order-execution APIs. None offers a paper-trading sandbox — which turns out not to matter, because this system simulates fills in its own database rather than routing them through a broker’s paper account.

The real blockers are data, not execution. There is no free equivalent to the US news API this project uses, and no Indian counterpart to the historical headline corpus that made backtesting possible. Both the sentiment component and the backtest plan need new sourcing before anything can be measured.

Carried over from V1

Two measurements were left unrun, both deferred on cost rather than on doubt. Each would sharpen a conclusion already drawn; neither changes the headline, which is why neither blocked V1 from closing.

  • Separate the score node from the prompt change. The final sentiment run altered two things at once — the node and the Portfolio Manager prompt that reads it — so its heavy trading cannot be attributed to either. One ablation with the new prompt and a neutral score settles it. About $0.87.
  • Test a second symbol. Every result here is AAPL. Whether any result here describes the strategy or one stock in one period is untested. The headline data for 108 candidate symbols is already prepared and sitting in the repository. About $0.87 per symbol.

Both are documented in the repository with the exact commands, so they are picked up rather than re-derived — by me when there is budget, or by anyone who wants to run them first.

The order it has to happen in, for V3

  • Source Indian historical headlines, then check coverage month by month — year-level totals once hid a five-month gap that made an entire backtest uninformative.
  • Source daily bars for an NSE universe over the same window.
  • Re-run the honest backtest before building anything on top of it. The V1 gate exists precisely so that a non-edge is not scaled up.

The continuous-intraday work, now V4, stays on hold. Running a strategy faster does not create an edge it does not have — and that gate was written before the data existed to test it, which is the only reason it was believed when it fired.

About

Pranav Thota

Software engineer

I built this out of curiosity. I wanted to find out whether a language model could actually read financial news and make a sensible call from it, and I wanted to build the whole thing properly — end to end, running on real data — rather than stopping at a notebook that half works.

The trading question turned out to be the less interesting half. The more interesting half was working out how you would know.

The part I am most pleased with is not the code. It is that the project set a rule at the start — back-test honestly, and report whatever comes back — and then held to it when the answer was negative. Two results in here looked like successes until they were checked against a trivial baseline. One statistical threshold turned out to sit below the resolving power of the test I had chosen for it. All of that is written down rather than smoothed over, because a system you can trust the negative results from is the only kind whose positive results mean anything.
